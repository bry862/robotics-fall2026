Across all three missions, I learned that finding the shortest route is not necessarily enough for a robot that shares space with people. The robot also needs to be able to navigate successfully, avoid obstacles, and maintain a reasonable distance from people. In Mission 1, BFS and A* both find shortest paths, but they approach the problem differently. BFS explores cells level by level, while A* uses a heuristic to prioritize cells that are closer to the goal. This can reduce the number of cells explored and the time needed to find a route. DFS can find a valid route, but it does not guarantee the shortest one. IDDFS can also be expensive because it repeats the search with increasing depth limits. In Mission 2, I learned that finding a path does not guarantee that the robot will successfully follow it. For example, on the constrained map, A* succeeded while BFS experienced a controller failure. On the open map, both succeeded, but A* traveled 13.155 m in 46.3 seconds, compared to BFS at 13.172 m in 46.5 seconds. Nav2 also performed well on the open map, traveling 10.084 m in 35.398 seconds. This shows that we need to consider how well the robot actually follows a route, not just how efficiently the planner finds it.
However, these results are estimates, not perfect measurements of what happened physically. For example, the robot's position comes from AMCL and odometry, and person clearance is calculated from estimated positions and body dimensions. LiDAR distance is also only a proximity measurement, not proof of a collision.
